# Business model and market: collaborative, industry-specific AI creative tool suites (film + games), state as of November 2026

Research method note: all findings below come from web-search result summaries gathered 8 Nov 2026. Direct fetches of company pricing pages (runwayml.com, higgsfield.ai, lumalabs.ai, meshy.ai, scenario.com, inworld.ai, frame.io, krea.ai, pika.art) and of several primary sources (fortnite.com, unrealengine.com, sec.gov, gdconf.com) were blocked by the session's network allowlist, and the shared web-search budget ran out before every sub-topic could be covered. Where a number comes only from a third-party pricing aggregator rather than the vendor's page, it is marked "(aggregator)". Where I could not find any source, the item is listed under Gaps rather than filled from memory. Facts are dated where the source gave a date.

## Key question 1: Which comparable companies have found revenue, and what pricing do they charge?

### Takeaway
A small number of multi-model "AI studio" aggregators have reached real scale (Higgsfield company-reported $700M annualised revenue Aug 2026; Runway $200-300M ARR; Freepik/Magnific $230M ARR bootstrapped; Meshy $100M ARR Sept 2026), all on credit-based monthly subscriptions in the roughly $10-$130/month range with a free tier. Most film-side "AI studios" (Promise, Asteria, Staircase, Secret Level, Toonstar, Primordial Soup) are production companies, not tool vendors, and have disclosed no revenue; most game-side tool startups (Scenario, Rosebud, Series, Ludo, Latitude, Hidden Door, Spellbrush) have raised under $30M and disclose no revenue.

### Inferences
- Revenue at scale correlates with (1) consumer/prosumer reach via a free tier plus $10-30 entry plans, and (2) aggregating many third-party models under one credit wallet (Higgsfield, Freepik/Magnific, Krea, Luma Agents), rather than with owning a model. The exception is Meshy, which owns its model but sells into a game/3D niche with few substitutes.
- Film-side "AI studios" are content companies; their disclosed capital is small or undisclosed, and none has shown recurring software revenue. A tools startup should not benchmark on them for pricing, only for partnership patterns (studio equity stakes, model-vendor partnerships).
- Price points cluster: entry $9-20, mid $25-60, top $75-130/month for individuals; team tiers put 15-25 seats on one shared credit pool at $45-75/month (Scenario Max, Meshy Studio) rather than charging per seat, which is notably cheaper than Frame.io's $25/user.

### Gaps
- No vendor pricing page could be fetched directly; every plan price above is from aggregators or vendor help-center snippets and should be re-checked.
- No revenue for Pika, Krea (beyond estimates), Scenario, Inworld, Rosebud, Series, Spellbrush, Ludo, Latitude, Hidden Door, or any film-side studio.
- Pika pricing and Krea pricing were not retrievable.
- 2026 status of Hidden Door and Latitude (launch, pivot, shutdown) not found.
- Moonvalley funding and whether Asteria formally merged with Moonvalley: unverified.

## Key question 2: How do multi-industry platforms (Adobe, Autodesk, Canva, Epic) serve several industries with one core plus vertical layers, and how do they price per vertical?

### Takeaway
Evidence gathered here is partial: Epic prices Unreal by industry (free plus 5% royalty for games vs $1,850/seat/year for non-game companies over $1M revenue), and Autodesk bundles its AI VFX tool (Flow Studio Pro) into the Media & Entertainment Collection. Searches for Adobe Creative Cloud 2026 tiers and Canva's vertical pricing could not be run before the search budget ran out.

### Cited Findings
- Epic's non-game pricing (announced March 2024, effective with UE 5.4, April 2024): $1,850 per seat per year for companies with over $1M annual gross revenue that are not making games; regional pricing in select countries; non-game apps licensed to third parties that use engine code at runtime pay royalties like games; Epic Pro Support not included and sold at $1,500 per seat per year for 10+ seats — [Unreal Engine blog](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/blog/we-are-updating-unreal-engine-twinmotion-and-realitycapture-pricing-in-late-april); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/epic-will-charge-non-game-developers-1850-per-seat-to-use-unreal-engine-162015997.html); [Cartoon Brew](https://cartoonbrew.com/tools/epic-games-unveils-new-per-seat-pricing-for-non-game-developers-using-unreal-engine-238987.html)

## Key question 3: What are realistic unit costs of generation that a reseller must cover, and what margins do credit-based resellers run?

### Takeaway
Wholesale costs as of mid/late 2026: roughly $0.25-$0.75 per 5-second 720p-1080p video clip on mainstream models (Veo 3.1 Lite $0.25, Kling 3.0 Standard $0.42, Runway Gen-4.5 $0.60, Veo 3.1 Fast $0.50-0.75), about $2 per 5 seconds on Veo 3.1 Standard, about $0.015-$0.04 per image, and about $0.13-$0.60 per textured 3D model. AI-app gross margins are widely reported at 50-60% vs 70-90% for classic SaaS, which is why nearly every reseller uses metered credits, rollover limits and "relax/slow" queues.

### Cited Findings
**Video**
- Veo 3.1 Lite: $0.05/sec at 720p, $0.08/sec at 1080p, no 4K — [vo3ai](https://www.vo3ai.com/blog/veo-31-lite-pricing-explained-is-googles-005second-ai-video-the-cheapest-api-opt-2026-04-01); [benchlm snapshot 11 Sep 2026](https://benchlm.ai/media-pricing/veo)

- Inworld Realtime TTS-2 $25 per million characters on demand down to $10 at enterprise scale; TTS 1.5 Mini $5; at about 750 characters per minute of audio, $10/M chars is about $0.0075 per minute and $25/M is about $0.019 per minute — [Inworld TTS](https://inworld.ai/tts?via=moritz); [Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/how-to-compare-tts-pricing-across-providers-2026)

**Margins**
- AI product gross margins typically 50-60% vs 80-90% for traditional SaaS; Bessemer documents 50-60% vs 70-90% — [Beancount guide 13 Jul 2026](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/13/llm-api-cost-cogs-ai-wrapper-gross-margin-guide); [SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/why-ai-gross-margins-are-so-much-lower-than-saas-and-what-that-means-for-your-business/)
- Heavy users can cost 50-100x more to serve than light users on the same tier; recommendation is metering or credits to cap the heavy-user problem; one case found 60% of inference cost from redundant requests (caching lever) — [Beancount](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/13/llm-api-cost-cogs-ai-wrapper-gross-margin-guide); [Dodo Payments](https://dodopayments.com/blogs/ai-wrapper-business-model-monetization/)
- Freepik/Magnific is described as bootstrapped and profitable at $230M ARR while reselling third-party video models — [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/freepik-rebrands-as-magnific)
- Scenario gives Enterprise a 30% compute discount vs standard tiers, implying list credit prices carry at least that much headroom — [Scenario help](https://help.scenario.com/articles/8751853402-scenario-for-enterprise)

### Inferences
- Worked example from the cited numbers: a $20/month plan that must deliver, say, 40 five-second clips on Kling 3.0 Standard ($0.42 each = $16.80 wholesale) leaves about 16% gross margin before storage and overhead; the same budget on Veo 3.1 Lite ($0.25 each = $10) leaves 50%. This is why aggregators route cheap "draft" generations to Lite/Turbo models and reserve premium models for higher tiers or extra credits.
- 3D is cheaper per asset than video per clip (about $0.13-$0.60 per model), so a game-vertical tier can offer more generations per dollar than a film-vertical tier; pricing the two verticals identically would under-monetize one or over-cost the other.
- Model vendors' own consumer plans sometimes undercut their API price per clip (Kling example), so a reseller cannot compete on raw price for single-model users; the value must come from workflow, collaboration and multi-model routing.

### Gaps
- No source gave an actual gross margin for Runway, Higgsfield, Krea or Luma; the 50-60% figure is a category benchmark.
- "Reseller/channel margin" specifically (e.g., what fal.ai or Replicate mark up) was not found.
- Official Google, Runway, Kling, Meshy and Tripo pricing pages were unreachable; all unit costs are from third-party trackers dated 2026.

## Key question 5: What 2026 legal and regulatory facts must a founder plan around?

### Takeaway
As of Nov 2026: the US Copyright Office's Part 3 training report (May 2025) remains a "pre-publication" document of contested status; Disney/Universal/WBD v. Midjourney is in discovery with trial timelines pointing to 2027; Getty v. Stability (UK) is on appeal after Getty lost its main copyright claims; EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duties applied from 2 Aug 2026 with only the machine-readable marking duty for pre-existing systems deferred to 2 Dec 2026; Steam narrowed AI disclosure to player-consumed content (Jan 2026); Epic's Fab requires a "CreatedWithAI" tag; Apple requires explicit consent before sharing personal data with third-party AI (Nov 2025); and SAG-AFTRA's 2025 Interactive Media Agreement governs digital replicas in games through 2027/2028.

### Cited Findings
**US Copyright Office**
- Part 3 (Generative AI Training) released as pre-publication 9 May 2025; says no substantive changes expected before final publication; Register of Copyrights fired the next day, leading commentators to question whether it is official guidance; concludes training and deployment involve acts that absent license or defense may infringe, treats fair use as central, says foundation-model training on diverse data "will often be transformative" but leans toward limiting fair use where there is market harm, including the market for training data — [Mishcon](https://www.mishcon.com/news/us-copyright-office-report-part-3-generative-ai-training); [Lowenstein](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/us-copyright-office-releases-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-and-copyright-law); [Mass Law Blog](https://masslawblog.com/general/copyright-office-issues-long-awaited-report-on-generative-ai-training-register-of-copyrights-is-fired-next-day/)
- No 2026 source found confirming a final Part 3 or any withdrawal.

**Disney/Universal (and WBD) v. Midjourney (C.D. Cal.)**
- Filed June 2025; Midjourney's Aug 2025 response asserts fair use; WBD sued Sept 2025 and the cases were combined Nov 2025; court suggested mediation Nov 2025 with proposed timelines running into 2027; 16 June 2026 magistrate order required the studios to produce some data on their own AI use while limiting Midjourney's broader discovery; no merits ruling found — [Law360 case page](https://www.law360.com/cases/6849920dbaefe0fe54b77b08); [Law360 discovery article](https://law360.com/articles/2489948); [Graphic Policy 14 Nov 2025](https://graphicpolicy.com/2025/11/14/court-suggests-disney-v-midjourney-go-to-mediation/); [Tribune](https://tribune.com.pk/story/2560235/midjourney-responds-to-disney-and-universal-lawsuit-cites-fair-use-in-ai-copyright-case)

**Getty Images v. Stability AI (England and Wales)**
- High Court (Mrs Justice Joanna Smith) dismissed Getty's copyright infringement claims (judgment dated 4 Nov 2025 in most sources); trade mark infringement found in part; Getty granted permission on 16 Dec 2025 to appeal the secondary-infringement finding (whether an AI model must store a copy to be an "infringing copy"), judge said appeal has "a real prospect of success"; Stability refused permission to appeal the trade mark finding; Getty ordered to pay about £4.4M interim costs; status "appeal pending" — [Burges Salmon](https://www.burges-salmon.com/articles/102lydc/getty-images-v-stability-ai-getty-granted-permission-to-appeal); [Chambers](https://chambers.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-remain-in-focus-for-2026-with-getty-appeal-given-the-green-light); [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2423073/getty-granted-appeal-bid-in-uk-ai-images-dispute-but-costs-mount); [Hogan Lovells tracker](https://digital-client-solutions.hoganlovells.com/resources/ai-litigation-case-law-tracker/case/getty-images-us-inc-v-stability-ai-ltd)

### Inferences
- A film+games suite must build provenance/labelling into its export path now: EU Article 50(2) marking (watermark/metadata) for all newly placed systems since 2 Aug 2026, Steam's player-facing disclosure, Fab's CreatedWithAI tag, and Apple's consent screen if a mobile client sends user content to third-party models. These are product features, not just legal text.
- Because the US fair-use question is unresolved (Copyright Office leaning restrictive, Midjourney trial not before 2027), vendors advertising "licensed-only" training data (Moonvalley/Marey used by Asteria) have a studio-sales advantage; a tool suite should expose model provenance per generation so studio customers can choose.
- Model deprecation and price swings within 12 months argue for a model-abstraction layer and for selling credits that are denominated in the suite's own units, not a specific vendor's.

## Key question 6: Demand side — how many people want to make films/games but cannot, and what will indie filmmakers and game developers pay for?

### Takeaway
UGC game platforms show large, paying creator bases: Roblox paid over 23,500 creators fiat in 2025 (over $1B via DevEx per secondary reports) and Epic has paid UEFN creators over $1B cumulatively since 2023 (about $352M in 2024, 70,000 creators, 198,000 islands). Steam saw a record 19,000-21,500 releases in 2025 with nearly half getting fewer than 10 reviews, and 36% of game professionals use generative AI while 52% say it harms the industry. Direct survey evidence on what indies would pay for in collaboration/automation was not found; the only filmmaker survey located (146 documentary makers, 2025) found almost three-quarters plan to use AI.

### Inferences
- The paying creator population in games is in the tens of thousands (Roblox 23,500 paid; Fortnite 70,000 creators in 2024) on top of hundreds of thousands of publishers and millions of hobbyists, and the supply glut on Steam (about 20k releases/year, half near-invisible) means the scarce inputs are discoverability, polish and finishing, not generation volume. Collaboration and production-management features address finishing; raw generation is commoditized.
- Developer sentiment is a go-to-market constraint: with 52% of game professionals negative on generative AI and only 5% using it in player-facing content, a game-vertical product that emphasizes team coordination, pipeline integration and disclosure compliance (Steam/Fab) is more sellable to studios than one marketed as "AI makes your game."
- Documentary/indie filmmakers report high intent to use AI (about 3/4 in a small 2025 sample) but the evidence base is thin; there is no located study quantifying what they would pay for collaboration or automation.
